## Ongoing projects

<section class="research-project" aria-labelledby="reveal">
  <div>
    <h3 id="reveal">REVEAL</h3>
    <p class="project-topic"><span>Genomic erosion and extinction risk</span></p>
    <p>Can genomic diversity, inbreeding and deleterious variation help predict extinction risk? I combine publicly available bird genomes, population-genetic simulations and machine learning to test how these relationships vary with life history. I also ask when a single reference genome can approximate estimates based on multiple individuals.</p>

<section class="research-project" aria-labelledby="italian-vertebrates">
  <div>
    <h3 id="italian-vertebrates">Whole-genome sequencing of Italian terrestrial vertebrates</h3>
    
    <p>We plan to sample around 40 individuals from each of 15–20 terrestrial vertebrate species across Italy and sequence their genomes at more than 30× coverage. We will build de novo reference genomes for five species and estimate genetic diversity and population structure across the dataset.</p>

<section class="research-project" aria-labelledby="expansion-load">
  <div>
    <h3 id="expansion-load">Climate change, range expansion and genetic load</h3>
    <p class="project-topic"><i>Bombina pachypus</i></p>
    <p>The Apennine yellow-bellied toad expanded northward after the last glaciation. We will use its genomes to estimate expansion load, the burden of deleterious variants associated with colonization. Spatially explicit simulations will test how the pace of climate change and colonization speed influence that load and the likelihood of a successful range expansion.</p>
  </div>

<section class="research-project" aria-labelledby="apennine-brown-bear">
  <div>
    <h3 id="apennine-brown-bear">Genomic diversity and local adaptation in the Apennine brown bear</h3>
    <p class="project-topic"><i>Ursus arctos marsicanus</i></p>
    <p>We will compare whole genomes from Apennine brown bears and other brown bear populations to reconstruct demographic history and identify variants that distinguish the Apennine population. We will examine regulatory regions and test for signatures of local adaptation to help inform conservation.</p>

<section class="research-project" aria-labelledby="hermanns-tortoise">
  <div>
    <h3 id="hermanns-tortoise">Conservation genomics of Hermann’s tortoise</h3>
    <p class="project-topic"><i>Testudo hermanni</i></p>
    <p>We will build a reference genome and compare whole-genome data from both subspecies to reconstruct their divergence, identify past bottlenecks and locate populations with low diversity or high inbreeding. We will also examine genes involved in immunity and sensory function to help assess the risks of translocation and hybridization.</p>

<section class="research-project" aria-labelledby="secretarybird">
  <div>
    <h3 id="secretarybird">Secretarybird conservation genomics</h3>
    <p class="project-topic"><i>Sagittarius serpentarius</i></p>
    <p>We will use a chromosome-level reference genome to assess diversity, inbreeding and genetic load in secretarybirds from Tanzania and South Africa. We will examine population structure to help identify conservation units, evaluate options for corridors and translocations, and establish a baseline for long-term monitoring.</p>

<section class="research-project" aria-labelledby="fiasco">
  <div>
    <h3 id="fiasco">FIASCO: Why some colonizations succeed and others fail</h3>
    <p class="project-topic"><i>Littorina saxatilis</i></p>
    <p>We will compare repeated successful and failed colonizations in the rough periwinkle. By combining whole-genome data with trait measurements and ecological data, we will test how demographic history, local conditions and phenotypic variation relate to whether populations establish and persist.</p>

## Published projects

<section class="research-project" aria-labelledby="fin-whales">
  <div>
    <h3 id="fin-whales">Fin whale population genomics</h3>
    <p class="project-topic"><i>Balaenoptera physalus</i></p>
    <p>We compared genomes from Mediterranean, Atlantic and Pacific fin whales to reconstruct population history and assess connectivity. Our results show that Mediterranean fin whales are genetically distinct, with evidence of connectivity with the North Atlantic.</p>

<section class="research-project" aria-labelledby="australian-lungfish">
  <div>
    <h3 id="australian-lungfish">The origins of Australian lungfish populations</h3>
    <p class="project-topic"><i>Neoceratodus forsteri</i></p>
    <p>We used genetic data and demographic modelling to revisit historical translocations. The results suggest lungfish were already present in some rivers where they had been assumed to be introduced, with implications for how those populations are managed.</p>
