I study how demographic history and evolutionary processes shape the genomes of wild populations, and how genomic evidence can inform conservation decisions.

<h2 id="reveal">REVEAL</h2>

My Marie Skłodowska-Curie fellowship at the University of Copenhagen asks whether genomic erosion can help predict extinction risk across species. I focus on changes in genetic diversity, inbreeding, and deleterious variation, and test whether their relationship with extinction risk depends on species’ life histories.

I combine publicly available bird genomes with population-genetic simulations and machine learning. I also test when a single reference genome can approximate measures estimated from multiple individuals. This matters for conservation because population-level sequencing is often limited for the species we most want to assess.

## Selected projects

### Population genomics of fin whales

We compared fin whales from the Mediterranean, Atlantic, and Pacific to reconstruct their demographic histories and examine population structure and genetic diversity. The study assessed how distinct Mediterranean fin whales are and what their genomic variation can tell us about connectivity with the Atlantic. [Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1093/gbe/evag084).

### Reference genomes for conservation

I work on chromosome-level genome assemblies that support studies of genome evolution and conservation. For the Adriatic sturgeon, a critically endangered polyploid fish, we generated a reference genome for studying chromosome organization and genome duplication. I also work on assembling and annotating the secretarybird genome. [Read the sturgeon paper](https://doi.org/10.1093/jhered/esag023).

### Reconstructing the history of Australian lungfish populations

We used population genetic data and demographic modelling to investigate the origins of Australian lungfish populations in rivers affected by historical translocations. [Read the paper](https://doi.org/10.1111/mec.17266).
